# DESIGN-GRAMMAR-LED GENERATION

The client's references were analyzed upstream and may also be attached for visual understanding. They are not editable source artwork.

AUTHORITY ORDER IS MANDATORY: `brandCI` → approved brand identity → `campaignIdea` → reference `designGrammar`. Every output must follow the client's Brand CI for colors, logo treatment, and font identity. If a reference conflicts with Brand CI on those specifically, Brand CI wins without compromise.

Use the reference for artwork-concept logic, key-visual mechanism, composition, hierarchy, scale, interaction, spatial relationships, information density, and secondary/footer structure. Re-express those ideas naturally through the client's Brand CI. Never import the reference's brand colors, fonts, logo treatment, or other recognizable brand identity — but do import its mood, photographic or illustration treatment, color grade, lighting signature, and material styling exactly as `designGrammar` describes them; those are meant to carry through, not to be filtered out. This distinction has a specific failure mode to avoid: if the reference's light source or glow reads as warm, keep it warm even though the brand's palette is cool (or vice versa) — a light's color temperature is part of the reference's mood, not part of the brand's color palette, and forcing every glow or highlight to match the brand's exact hue is what turns a warm, human mood into a cold generic-tech one.

Create an original advertisement whose hero visual clearly expresses `campaignIdea`. Preserve only the concept and key-visual rules listed in `designGrammar.preserve`. Replace everything listed in `designGrammar.replace`.

Do not reconstruct the source advertisement's people, products, scene, props, or exact composition. Treat any images still attached to this request only according to their labels, such as official logo or product identity.

Render only the supplied `visibleCopy`: headline, at most one optional supporting line, and CTA. Context fields and user instructions are not visible copy. Do not add explanatory copy, labels, feature lists, contact details, or decorative text.

Use only the supplied approved copy and attached brand assets.

CHOOSE THE EXECUTION HONESTLY: a real photograph is not automatically the right answer. Pick whichever of these genuinely fits this brand and this concept, the same way a senior art director would — a believable photograph, a flat/gradient studio field with staged elements composited onto it (the normal, professional choice for most consideration/conversion work and often the more premium one), or a consistent illustrated style (mandatory when the hero is inherently fictional — a mascot, a character — or when the brand's own identity is illustration-led). Some categories have their own established convention regardless of this list — an unbuilt property is legitimately shown as an architectural render, for instance. Whatever is chosen, commit to it fully rather than drifting into an undefined, overly rendered "AI 3D" look that is neither real photography nor deliberate illustration.

If the execution is photographic: use a physically believable camera position, focal length and perspective — think in terms of an actual camera and lens, not "photorealistic" as a vague label. Define one primary real-world light source and realistic environmental bounce; every highlight, reflection and shadow must be explainable by it. Preserve controlled imperfections — subtle material irregularity, natural texture, imperfect reflections, slight asymmetry, varied surface detail — rather than smoothing everything to a plastic sheen. People should read as a specific real person of a specific age, not an ageless, poreless, symmetrical default face, and should hold a natural, unposed moment rather than a rehearsed smile.

ANTI-AI AESTHETIC — these are brand-agnostic defaults every model reaches for when nothing constrains it; block them regardless of which brand or color this campaign uses:
- No generic color-glow halo, aura, or gradient orb floating in the scene purely for decoration — this applies to any hue (a brand-blue glow is exactly as much a cliché as a purple one); a light source must justify itself physically or narratively, not just make the image "look techy/premium."
- No monumental or architectural scale applied to a metaphor by default (giant buildings, towering monuments, oversized structures) when the concept works at human or object scale instead — reach for architectural scale only when the idea genuinely demands it, not as the reflexive way to look "epic."
- No particles, ribbons, mist, floating icons, glassmorphism cards, cloud shapes, a 3D pedestal/plinth (unless the product is genuinely staged on one), or miniature people added simply to make the image visually richer.
- No overly perfect composition, overly polished/glossy plastic surfaces, identical lighting on every element, fake HDR, excessive bokeh, unnecessary rim light, cinematic haze, unexplained/unshadowed floating objects, mathematically distributed decoration, or a generic gradient background reached for as the safe default.
- Do not let small background text (signage, screens, spines, distant labels) appear if it cannot be rendered legibly — omit or abstract it rather than letting it come out garbled, which is one of the fastest tells that an image is synthetic.
- Do not default to a permanently dark, neon-on-dark surface with glowing card borders, nor to a generic warm cream/beige backdrop, as the automatic "safe, tasteful" choice — pick the surface this specific brand and concept calls for.

The result should feel like a senior designer shot and composited this for real — production-ready agency key visual — not like an AI model generated an attractive advertising scene.
