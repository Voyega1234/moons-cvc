---

# PRIMARY DESIGN STANDARD

Create an editorially art-directed key visual that can perform as advertising.

The artwork must first succeed as a visually strong image before supporting
commercial information is added.

A simple idea executed with expert visual judgment is better than a complicated
concept filled with explanatory elements.

Prioritize:

- visual taste
- composition
- confident scale
- typography
- lighting
- material quality
- controlled color
- believable depth
- restraint
- production-level finishing

Do not illustrate every sentence in the brief.

Decide what to omit.

Default to editorial restraint unless the campaign explicitly requires dense
retail, marketplace, promotional, legal, price, date, or multi-product
information.

A conversion objective does not automatically require a crowded image.

The final result must feel designed, not assembled.

It must not resemble:

- stock photography with text added afterward
- separate PNG assets pasted onto a background
- an infographic explaining the business problem
- a presentation slide
- a brochure compressed into one image
- a generic AI advertising template

---

# RESOLVE ONE COHERENT ART DIRECTION

Before rendering, reason internally and commit to one visual system.

Resolve:

- one dominant visual proposition
- one hero or deliberately unified hero group
- one stage and background treatment
- one composition logic
- one typography system
- one lighting thesis
- one material language
- one color hierarchy
- one reading order
- one appropriate density level

Do not combine several unrelated design styles.

Do not include every possible commercial design device merely because it is
available.

Every visible element must belong to the same visual world.

---

# VISUAL PROPOSITION

Choose one visually memorable proposition.

It may use:

- a real object
- a product
- a portrait
- a material interaction
- an unusual scale relationship
- a confident crop
- an atmospheric scene
- a restrained surreal intervention
- a typographic composition
- a simple relationship between two elements

It does not need to literally illustrate every word.

Prefer:

- suggestion over explanation
- visual tension over complete storytelling
- one strong relationship over many symbols
- one unusual but elegant image over a staged business scenario
- confident execution over conceptual complication

The image should communicate intuitively.

Do not create a scene that requires arrows, labels, callouts, handwritten
annotations, warning cards, or explanatory sentences to identify what each
object represents.

When the visual needs a note such as:

- “this is the real customer”
- “this is the blockage”
- “this represents bad leads”
- “this is the point where the problem happens”

reject the execution and simplify it.

The headline may sharpen the meaning, but it must not function as an
instruction manual for understanding the image.

---

# BACKGROUND AND STAGE

Design the background as an intentional visual stage.

Clean does not mean blank.

A strong background may use:

- controlled material texture
- subtle atmospheric gradients
- architecture or spatial planes
- fabric, paper, stone, metal, glass, wall, floor, or environmental surfaces
- light falloff
- restrained environmental context
- foreground or background depth

The background must:

1. establish the mood
2. support the hero
3. provide calm typography space
4. create believable depth

Keep the area behind important text visually quiet.

Background detail must remain lower in contrast and importance than the hero.

Do not fill empty space merely because it exists.

Avoid:

- random floating geometry
- meaningless decorative objects
- generic blue-purple AI gradients
- holographic rings
- excessive particles
- overcomplicated environments
- decorative props that do not support the communication

---

# COMPOSITION

Build the artwork from a small number of confident visual masses.

Use:

- one dominant hero
- one primary typography mass
- intentional negative space
- strong scale contrast
- confident cropping
- controlled overlap
- asymmetrical balance when appropriate
- clear focal and supporting areas

Do not make every element medium-sized.

Do not distribute attention evenly across the canvas.

Do not make the headline, hero, CTA, badge, and supporting copy compete at the
same visual weight.

Create depth through:

- scale
- overlap
- perspective
- foreground cropping
- atmospheric contrast
- light falloff
- selective sharpness
- contact shadows
- cast shadows

Do not create depth by adding unnecessary objects.

The focal area should have the strongest detail and edge definition.
Background areas should remain calmer and less sharp.

Avoid accidental tangencies, cramped corners, strange crops, and elements
that almost touch without a clear relationship.

---

# TYPOGRAPHY

Typography is part of the artwork, not an annotation layer.

Treat the headline as shape, mass, rhythm, and visual personality.

Choose a typographic character appropriate to the brand and campaign.
Use one coherent type system with only the necessary hierarchy.

Control:

- type character
- scale
- weight
- line breaks
- leading
- tracking
- alignment
- text-block width
- color emphasis
- relationship with the hero
- placement within the composition

Use strong scale contrast.

The headline must have noticeably greater authority than supporting copy.

Typography can feel dimensional through:

- scale
- contrast
- placement
- overlap
- proximity
- tonal separation
- spatial relationship with the hero

Do not automatically use:

- bevel
- extrusion
- thick outlines
- several drop shadows
- excessive glow
- multiple effect styles

Use dimensional promotional typography only when the campaign is explicitly
retail, event-driven, youthful, entertainment-focused, festive, or
marketplace-led.

For Thai typography:

- preserve the exact approved wording
- use natural Thai line breaks
- keep important phrases together
- never break words awkwardly
- maintain readability at mobile-feed size
- avoid effects that deform Thai letterforms
- do not invent or rewrite copy

Render mandatory copy exactly once.

---

# INFORMATION RESTRAINT

By default, use only:

- one official logo
- one headline
- optionally one short supporting line
- optionally one restrained CTA
- mandatory legal, price, date, offer, or contact information

Use approved optional information only when it materially improves
understanding or persuasion without weakening the key visual.

Do not repeat the same meaning through:

- the hero image
- an explanatory label
- a warning card
- a paragraph
- a benefit list
- icon rows
- multiple proof blocks

When the visual already communicates the point, allow the copy to confirm it
rather than explain it again.

Do not turn the lower third into:

- a feature checklist
- a process explanation
- a presentation panel
- a brochure footer
- a collection of trust badges
- a large glossy CTA section

Secondary information belongs in the caption, next carousel frame, landing
page, or campaign copy unless explicitly required on the artwork.

The CTA must be visible when mandatory, but it must not overpower the headline
or hero.

For retail or marketplace campaigns that genuinely require more information,
organize it into a small number of clear information groups with one obvious
reading order. Do not make every offer equally loud.

---

# HUMAN PRESENCE

Use people only when a person is genuinely the strongest editorial subject or
materially improves the campaign communication.

Do not add people merely to act out a business pain point.

Avoid generic scenes such as:

- a worried owner holding their head
- a person pointing toward a metaphorical object
- someone operating an oversized symbolic machine
- a customer waiting outside while another person struggles inside
- actors performing the headline literally
- exaggerated stock-photo emotions

When a person is used:

- use a purposeful pose
- preserve natural anatomy and hands
- maintain believable skin texture
- avoid waxy skin
- avoid wet-looking highlights
- avoid excessive facial sharpening
- integrate the person naturally into the same lighting and environment

---

# LIGHT, MATERIAL, AND DEPTH

Choose one clear lighting thesis appropriate to the brand and subject.

Define internally:

- key-light direction
- softness or hardness
- fill level
- shadow direction
- brightest point
- darkest point
- color temperature
- atmospheric quality

All primary elements must exist in the same lighting world.

Lighting must reveal form, texture, and material.

Render visible materials according to their real physical behavior.

Preserve believable differences between:

- matte and glossy
- soft and rigid
- transparent and opaque
- reflective and diffuse
- smooth and porous

Use believable:

- contact shadows
- cast shadows
- reflections when appropriate
- object-to-surface interaction
- scale
- perspective
- edge behavior
- atmospheric integration

Avoid:

- universal plastic gloss
- inconsistent shadows
- unexplained reflections
- arbitrary rim lighting
- excessive bloom
- equally sharp detail across the entire image
- pasted or floating assets
- physically confusing metaphors

---

# COLOR

Build a disciplined color hierarchy from the supplied brand truth.

Use:

- one dominant base
- one supporting tonal family
- one controlled accent role
- appropriate neutral space
- coherent highlight and shadow temperature

Accent color must have a specific function, such as:

- the hero
- one key word
- the product
- the offer
- the CTA
- one critical detail

Do not distribute accent color everywhere.

Make all supplied and generated elements share a coherent color grade.

Editorial and premium work should generally use restrained saturation and
controlled tonal relationships.

Promotional work may use stronger color, but must still maintain one dominant
temperature and clear hierarchy.

---

# BRAND AND ASSET ACCURACY

Preserve official assets exactly.

Do not:

- redesign the logo
- modify packaging
- distort labels
- invent product variants
- alter certifications
- change identifying details
- treat a logo asset as a visual-style reference unless explicitly instructed

Integrate official assets through believable:

- scale
- perspective
- lighting
- shadow
- color temperature
- spatial placement

Use reference images to understand:

- visual taste
- composition
- scale
- crop
- negative space
- typography
- lighting
- material quality
- density
- finishing

Do not copy a reference literally.
Do not reproduce its exact objects, scene, or layout.
Create an ownable visual appropriate to the active brand and campaign.

---

# REJECT GENERIC AI DESIGN

Do not rely on:

- literal business storytelling
- worried stock-photo people
- piles of repeated symbolic objects
- oversized metaphorical machines
- problem-versus-solution split scenes
- arrows explaining the image
- handwritten labels decoding the concept
- floating interface panels
- feature cards placed over the hero
- glossy rounded UI blocks
- icon rows
- large brochure-style footers
- excessive CTA treatments
- multiple competing focal points
- random surreal objects
- generic futuristic technology imagery
- overcomplicated compositions created mainly to appear impressive

Do not make the image look more sophisticated merely by adding more elements.

Prefer fewer, better-resolved decisions.

---

# FINAL INTERNAL QUALITY CHECK

Before rendering, evaluate the composition internally.

Revise it when:

- the image needs arrows or labels to be understood
- a person merely acts out the headline
- the composition resembles a brochure or presentation slide
- optional copy compensates for a weak visual
- the CTA competes with the hero
- every area contains information
- the background is busy without adding atmosphere
- typography appears pasted over the image
- scale, perspective, lighting, or materials are inconsistent
- the scene resembles stock photography mixed with generated props
- the image feels literal, generic, cheap, cluttered, or visibly AI-made
- removing optional copy causes the artwork to lose all meaning or visual value

The preferred result contains:

- one dominant visual proposition
- one coherent atmosphere
- one typographic system
- one clear visual tension
- only the information required to complete the communication
